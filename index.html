Overview
One workspace for functional safety, cybersecurity, SOTIF, and AI safety

FuCSAI is a model-based workspace for teams building safety-critical electrical, electronic, software, and AI-enabled systems. It brings consistent, standards-aligned analysis to every stage of the lifecycle — from early concept work through system, hardware, and software development, and on to production and operation.

Perspective
When AI Assistance Meets Model-Based Safety Engineering
Keeping pace with generative AI without losing traceability, review, or certification readiness

Generative AI can now draft analysis, documentation, and even code faster than any engineering team could type it by hand. In safety-critical work, that speed is a double-edged sword: an AI assistant that can generate a hazard list or a set of requirements in seconds is only useful if every one of those outputs can still be reviewed, traced, and justified the way the standard requires. The bottleneck doesn't disappear when generation gets faster — it just moves downstream, to verification, integration, and sign-off.

The question worth asking isn't whether AI assistance belongs in functional safety, cybersecurity, SOTIF, or AI safety work — it clearly does. The real question is how to bring it in without quietly trading away the rigor, traceability, and engineering accountability those standards exist to protect.

From Answering Questions to Working the Model

Most AI assistants today are conversational: you ask, they answer, and the conversation stays separate from the actual project. The more useful shift is an assistant that operates directly inside the same Model Browser you already work in — able to see the current item definition, the existing hazards and goals, and the structure of the work product you're building, and to propose or draft changes to that structure rather than just describing what you might do next. Grounding the assistant in the actual model, rather than a free-floating chat window, is what keeps its suggestions checkable against something concrete instead of just plausible-sounding text.

A Shared Model Gives AI Something to Be Checked Against

Model-based safety engineering — representing an item, its hazards, goals, and requirements as a connected, structured model rather than scattered documents — has always helped teams catch inconsistencies early. That structure becomes more valuable, not less, once AI is involved. A draft HARA entry, a proposed FMEA row, or a suggested safety requirement can be evaluated the same way any engineer's first draft would be: does it trace to a real hazard, does it conflict with an existing requirement, does it fit the architecture already defined. AI-assisted work products still have to earn their place in the model; the model doesn't relax its standards just because a draft arrived faster.

This plays out differently at each stage of the work. Early on, AI assistance is most useful for the repetitive first pass — turning a plain-language item description into a starting HARA structure, or drafting an initial set of safety goals from a hazard list, so the engineer's time goes into judgment calls rather than blank-page setup. Later, as work products move toward something a reviewer or assessor will actually sign off on, the bar rises: a draft has to go through the same review, traceability, and consistency checks as anything else in the project, regardless of whether a person or an assistant wrote the first version. AI doesn't get a shortcut through that gate, and it shouldn't.

Where It Actually Breaks (or Doesn't): Integration

Most real defects don't show up inside a single work product — they show up where disciplines meet: where a functional safety requirement interacts with a cybersecurity control, or where a SOTIF triggering condition depends on an assumption an AI safety analysis made about training data. Reviewing an AI-drafted change in isolation misses exactly the kind of problem that matters most. A shared, traceable model spanning all four disciplines is what makes it possible to actually see those cross-discipline effects before they surface downstream — which is the whole reason FuCSAI treats Functional Safety, Cybersecurity, SOTIF, and AI Safety as one connected workspace rather than four separate tools.

The Balance We're Building Toward

Teams that adopt AI assistance without enforcing traceability tend to pay for it later — in rework at integration, in slower certification, in documentation that doesn't hold up under audit. Teams that keep AI assistance anchored inside a structured, model-based workflow can move faster with real confidence, because every AI-assisted change stays connected to the hazard, goal, or requirement that justifies it. That's the balance FuCSAI is built around: assistance that drafts and suggests, a model that keeps everything traceable, and an engineer who stays firmly in charge of what actually ships.
